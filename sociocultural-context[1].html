Sociocultural Context

Research subpage | Abdufattoh Abdujabbarov

Food as cultural identity

Street food can express belonging through familiar flavours, preparation methods, ingredients, and eating rituals. A dish may connect people to a family memory, neighbourhood, ethnic community, or national story. However, no dish represents every member of a nation equally. Food identity is diverse and changes across generations and regions.

Public space and social interaction

Markets and street-food areas are places where people meet outside the home. Customers may include workers, students, families, older residents, and tourists. Shared tables and informal conversations can create everyday contact between people with different backgrounds. Singapore's hawker centres are officially described as community dining spaces that bring people from different backgrounds together (National Environment Agency, n.d.; UNESCO, 2020).

Social class and access

Affordable street food can make prepared meals accessible to people with different incomes. Yet affordability does not remove social inequality. Vendors may work long hours, face unstable earnings, and have limited access to secure premises or business support. Consumers may also experience food markets differently depending on income, mobility, location, and cultural familiarity.

Gender and family labour

Women and men contribute to street-food economies in different ways, although roles vary by country, community, and business. A field study of 50 vendors in Hanoi documented gendered livelihood strategies and showed that women's work was shaped by social networks, household relations, and unequal access to formal protection (Kawarazuka et al., 2018). Family businesses may rely on unpaid or under-recognized labour in preparation, purchasing, cleaning, serving, and accounting. Further research should avoid assuming that all women vendors have the same experiences or that street vending automatically produces empowerment.

Religion and diversity

Religious practices influence ingredients, preparation, and food choices. Halal requirements are important for many Muslim consumers and businesses in countries such as Indonesia and Malaysia. Other religious and cultural traditions also influence dietary practices across the region. Street-food markets can therefore reflect both shared public life and the need to respect different dietary requirements.

Sociocultural conclusion

Street food can strengthen community relationships and preserve cultural knowledge, but its benefits are not distributed equally. A critical analysis must consider whose traditions are represented, whose labour is visible, and who has access to safe and affordable food.
