Project Overview

Project purpose

This project investigates street food as a meeting point between food, culture, language, identity, economy, politics, and lifestyle in Southeast Asia. Rather than treating street food only as a collection of popular dishes, we examine it as a social practice shaped by historical exchange, migration, urban development, government decisions, tourism, and everyday consumer choices.

Research objectives

Our objectives are to: (1) explain the historical development of street-food cultures in selected Southeast Asian countries; (2) examine how food practices and public eating spaces express cultural identity and social relationships; (3) analyze the economic opportunities and vulnerabilities experienced by vendors; (4) consider how public policy and urban regulation affect street vending; (5) investigate how food names, menus, and multilingual communication carry cultural meanings; and (6) assess how tourism and globalization create both opportunities and pressures.

Scope and geographical focus

The project focuses on Southeast Asia, with comparative examples from Thailand, Vietnam, Malaysia, Indonesia, and Singapore. These cases are not intended to represent every country or community in the region. Instead, they provide a focused comparison of different histories, food traditions, urban settings, and policy approaches.

Approach and sources

We use secondary research, including peer-reviewed journal articles, official cultural-heritage information, government materials, and reputable institutional sources. We compare sources rather than relying on a single description of a country or dish. Academic claims are supported by in-text citations and a consolidated APA 7 reference list.

Central argument

Street food both preserves and changes culture. It can support livelihoods, create shared public spaces, and communicate identity through recipes and language. At the same time, vendors may face insecure income, uneven regulation, food-safety constraints, and pressure to adapt to tourism and commercial markets. A balanced understanding must recognize both its cultural value and the unequal conditions under which it operates.
