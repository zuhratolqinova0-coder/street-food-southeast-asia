Research method

This portfolio uses a comparative qualitative approach based on secondary sources. We examine academic research, institutional heritage information, and official public information to understand how street food connects to culture, language, identity, economy, politics, and lifestyle. The selected country cases are used to illustrate themes rather than to make claims about every community in Southeast Asia.

Source evaluation

Academic journal articles are prioritized for analytical claims. Institutional sources such as UNESCO are used for official heritage information. Each source should be checked for authorship, publication date, publisher, research method, geographical scope, and relevance to the claim it supports. A study of one city should not be described as evidence for an entire region unless its methods justify that scope.

Ethical considerations

Research about vendors and communities should avoid stereotypes and respect the people whose knowledge is being discussed. If the team adds interviews, photographs, or observations, it must obtain permission, explain the purpose, and avoid publishing identifiable information without consent.

Limitations

The project is limited by the availability and geographical coverage of published research. The selected five countries do not represent all Southeast Asian food cultures. The project also relies mainly on secondary sources and does not claim to have conducted original fieldwork. These limitations should be acknowledged in the final presentation.

Future improvement

A future version could include interviews with vendors, a comparative map of selected food markets, observations of menus and multilingual signs, and a small survey of consumer experiences. Such work would add primary evidence and help test the arguments developed in this portfolio.
