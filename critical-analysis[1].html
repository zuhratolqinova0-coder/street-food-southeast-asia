Critical analysis

Southeast Asian street food has cultural, social, and economic value, but it should not be romanticized. It can preserve recipes, provide affordable meals, and support livelihoods. At the same time, vendors may experience unstable income, insecure public-space access, limited infrastructure, and unequal treatment. A balanced analysis recognizes both contributions and vulnerabilities.

Culture versus commercialization

Tourism can increase demand for traditional dishes and create income. However, marketing may simplify local food cultures into a small number of famous dishes or encourage vendors to adapt food for visitor expectations. Cultural visibility does not automatically mean that local communities control how their heritage is represented or share equally in the benefits.

Regulation versus livelihood

Food-safety and urban-management policies can protect the public. Yet rules may have unequal effects when vendors cannot afford compliance or when enforcement is inconsistent. Pulliat et al. (2024) show why analysis must examine not only written regulations but also how rules are applied in everyday urban life.

Food safety as a structural issue

Food safety should not be framed only as an individual vendor's responsibility. Access to potable water, drainage, waste collection, refrigeration, and suitable public facilities depends partly on infrastructure and public investment. Soon-Sinclair et al. (2026) provide regional evidence that access to potable water remains a recurring challenge.

Research gaps

Further research is needed on: (1) gendered and unpaid family labour in small food businesses; (2) the experiences of migrant and minority vendors; (3) how digital delivery platforms affect income and autonomy; (4) differences between formal rules and actual enforcement; (5) the environmental effects of packaging and food waste; (6) how young people learn and reinterpret food traditions; and (7) how vendors themselves define authenticity and cultural heritage.

Our academic position

We argue that street food should be understood as a living cultural and economic system. Policies and tourism initiatives should protect food heritage while also supporting the people who produce it. Preservation should not mean freezing recipes or excluding vendors from public space. It should mean sustaining knowledge, livelihoods, safe working conditions, and community participation.

Limitations

This project is based primarily on secondary research and selected country cases. It does not claim to represent every Southeast Asian country or every vendor experience. Future work could include interviews with vendors and consumers, observations of markets, and comparisons across more countries and urban settings.
