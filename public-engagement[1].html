Public Engagement

Why public engagement matters

Research about street food should be useful beyond the classroom. Public engagement can help students, residents, visitors, and policymakers understand the cultural value of food traditions and the working conditions of vendors.

Proposed educational project: Street Food Stories

We propose a digital storytelling project that documents the stories behind selected street foods. Each entry could include the dish name in its original language, a short explanation of its meaning, its ingredients and preparation, the community or region associated with it, and a short interview with a vendor—only with informed consent.

Proposed learning activity

Students could compare food names across languages, map the locations of selected markets, and discuss how migration and trade influence food traditions. They could also examine how public policies affect vendors and identify practical ways to improve food safety without excluding small businesses.

Ethical principles

Any public-facing project should obtain permission before photographing or interviewing vendors. It should avoid stereotypes, respect cultural and religious differences, accurately translate names, and avoid presenting one person's experience as representative of an entire country. Personal information should not be published without consent.

Potential partners

Possible partners include local schools, cultural organizations, market associations, food-safety educators, tourism organizations, and community groups. Collaboration should be based on mutual benefit and should recognize vendors as knowledge holders.

Expected impact

Public engagement can make food heritage more visible, encourage respectful tourism, support intergenerational knowledge-sharing, and promote discussion of safe and inclusive public food spaces.
