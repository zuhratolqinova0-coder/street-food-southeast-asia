Singapore

Country case study

Hawker centres

Singapore's hawker centres are organized public eating spaces where independent food vendors prepare meals for residents and visitors. They are important parts of everyday urban life and provide a setting for people from different backgrounds to eat and interact. UNESCO inscribed Singapore's hawker culture on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2020 (UNESCO, 2020).

UNESCO recognition

Singapore's hawker culture was inscribed in 2020 on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. UNESCO describes hawker centres as community spaces where people from diverse backgrounds gather, eat, and mingle (UNESCO, 2020).

Cultural exchange

Hawker food reflects the interaction of Chinese, Malay, Indian, and other culinary traditions. Dishes have been adapted over time to local tastes and contexts. The result is not simply a collection of separate cuisines but a dynamic food culture shaped by everyday contact.

Heritage and livelihoods

Heritage recognition can increase visibility and encourage documentation, training, and public appreciation. However, sustaining hawker culture also depends on vendors' ability to continue working, transfer skills, manage costs, and attract new generations.

Policy and public infrastructure

Singapore's hawker-centre model illustrates how governments can organize food vending in designated facilities. It also raises questions about affordability, business succession, operating costs, and how cultural practices are maintained within regulated spaces.

Key insight

Singapore shows that cultural heritage is sustained not only through recognition but through the people, skills, spaces, and everyday practices that keep a food culture alive.
